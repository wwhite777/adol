Abstract
Reliable evaluation of large language models (LLMs) is critical as their de-
ployment rapidly expands, particularly in high-stakes domains. The LLM-
as-a-Judge framework, which uses prompted LLMs to evaluate response
quality, is appealing due to its scalability, low cost, and strong correlations
with human stylistic preferences. However, it remains unclear how accu-
rately these methods can assess response quality when correctness matters
more than style. To address this gap, we introduce the Business and Finance
Fundamentals Benchmark (BFF-Bench), a dataset of 160 challenging ques-
tions and long-form responses authored by financial professionals. These
experts subsequently evaluated the correctness of 1,200 responses gener-
ated by a diverse set of LLMs on both BFF-Bench and a challenging subset
of MT-Bench. With this expert-annotated dataset of judgments (VERDICTS),
we analyze the agreement between a suite of automated grading methods
and human experts. While we observe that LLM Judges are more reliable
than other grading methods, our findings reveal a clear pattern in LLM
Judge performance: when not provided with a correct reference, judges
show high agreement with human expertsonly on questions the judges were
able to correctly answer themselves. We demonstrate that providing the judges
with expert-written references largely mitigates this issue, highlighting the
limits of using LLM-as-a-Judge without any form of human verification.

1 Introduction
For text generation tasks such as translation, summarization, and question answering,
models provide free-form responses that are evaluated for some notion of overall quality
(Celikyilmaz et al., 2021). Because of the open-ended nature of the output space, automated
evaluation methods often rely on lexical overlap heuristics that compare model outputs
to trusted references (e.g., BLEU, Papineni et al., 2002; ROUGE, Lin, 2004; and METEOR,
Banerjee & Lavie, 2005) or—more recently—on methods such as reward models (Ouyang
et al., 2022; Kocmi & Federmann, 2023; Fu et al., 2024) and LLM-as-a-Judge (Zheng et al., 2023;
Li et al., 2025a). Using LLM-as-a-Judge for evaluation has become increasingly prominent
due to its flexibility and high correlation with human preference. Benchmarks such as
MT-Bench (Zheng et al., 2023), AlpacaEval2.0 (Dubois et al., 2024), ArenaHardAuto (Li et al.,
2025b), and WildBench (Lin et al., 2025) are commonly used to evaluate the capabilities of
frontier language models.
The LLM-as-a-Judge framework is typically used in the following way: a model that we
want to evaluate (thecandidate model) generates responses to a set of benchmark questions.
Then, to evaluate these responses, an LLM (thejudge modelorLLM Judge) is prompted to
produce some kind of quality score from each response. The candidate model’s response
can be graded in isolation (single grading) or in comparison to an additional candidate
model’s response (pairwise grading). Each of the aforementioned benchmarks validates
their use of LLM-as-a-Judge by measuring correlations with human preference annotations.
Human preferences can naturally vary along many different axes, making it difficult to

In some high-stakes domains such as business and finance, however, correctness outweighs
other attributes (Bommasani et al., 2022; Feuer et al., 2025; Krumdick et al., 2024). In these
settings, accurately judging response quality hinges on identifying whether a response is
correct, which itself can require complex reasoning and domain knowledge (Tan et al., 2025;
Zheng et al., 2023). In this work, we investigate the following question:Can LLMs make
reliable judgments of correctness, and therefore quality, in these high-stakes domains?
Our main contributions are as follows:
1. We create BFF-Bench: a new, challenging conversational benchmark with a focus
on correctness.This business and finance themed benchmark contains 80 conversa-
tions spanning 160 questions with long-form reference answers created and verified
by domain experts.
2. Using data from BFF-Bench and MT-Bench, we create theVerifiedExpertResponse
Dataset forIdentifyingCorrectness overTextStyle, or VERDICTS: a dataset of expert
judgments of correctness for 1,200 LLM responsesto evaluate the reliability of
methods of pairwise and single grading.
3. We demonstrate that on VERDICTS,whether an LLM Judge agrees with human
experts is strongly related to its ability to answer the underlying benchmark
question and the correctness of the provided reference(Figure 1).
2 Related Work
Limitations of LLM-as-a-Judge.A growing body of work evaluates the shortcomings
of LLM-as-a-Judge. Factors such as length, order, and tone of a response have all been
shown to bias LLM Judges’ evaluation (Zheng et al., 2023; Koo et al., 2024; Wei et al.,
2025). Benchmarks specifically designed to reveal such shortcomings have emphasized the
challenges of developing reliable LLM Judges (Koo et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2025). To mitigate
such biases and enhance agreement with human annotators, various strategies have been
proposed, including response-order randomization (Zheng et al., 2023), explicit debiasing

verified reference responses for LLM evaluation (Bai et al., 2024; Malaviya et al., 2024;

datasets with domain-specific questions, expert-verified long-form solutions, and annotated
reasoning errors. Szymanski et al. (2025) examines LLM-as-a-judge in the domains of
dietetics and mental health by comparing GPT-4’s ratings to those of domain professionals,
finding only moderate agreement on a dataset of 25 questions. In contrast, VERDICTS is the
only dataset to contain questions, long-form answers, and judgments of correctness, all
provided by the same domain experts.
3 Datasets
To use as little synthetic data as possible in constructing our core benchmarks, we worked
with human experts to create challenging questions with reference solutions, and annotate
response correctness. A more detailed description of our annotators and annotation process
can be found in Section A. We introduce a novel dataset ( BFF-Bench), a corrected subset
of an existing dataset ((C)MT-Bench), and a set of human expert labels of model response
correctness over these two datasets (VERDICTS).1
3.1BFF-Bench
Our goal was to collect a benchmark that was conversational in nature but with unambigu-
ous correctness. To have the same expertise available for both question creation and grading,

We collected 80 two-turn conversational questions with gold reference answers, all written

The question-response pairs were developed by a team of financial professionals. Each pair
underwent an iterative quality control process and verification. Questions were designed to
have unambiguous answers to facilitate unambiguous correctness judgments. Many require
mathematical manipulation, from simple arithmetic to the application of financial formulas,
whereas others test domain knowledge. Given their intended difficulty and technical scope,
each question-response pair required approximately one hour to create, clean, and verify.
3.2(C)MT-Bench
To increase the diversity of our judgment set, we collected data from the math and reasoning
subsets (40 total questions) from MT-Bench. This subset, unlike MT-Bench’s more open-
ended categories,2 is evaluated with references by default in the MT-Bench pipeline and
leads to responses that tend to be unambiguously correct or incorrect.
Inspired by Inflection AI (2024), we reviewed the provided GPT-4-generated reference
answers for these 40 questions andfound that 15 (37.5%) were either incorrect or inconsistent. 3
An example of one of these errors is provided in Figure 5 in Section B. We created correct
references for each question by manually re-writing and verifying the existing references.
These corrected references include the correct answer and a coherent reasoning chain,
detailing all necessary sub-steps. We refer to this corrected subset as(C)MT-Bench.

4 Experimental Setup
We experiment on two judgment tasks derived from our VERDICTS dataset, measuring
agreement with human annotators for both the standard LLM-as-a-Judge pipeline and a
collection of baselines. The final dataset composition is in Table 1.
4.1 Judgment Tasks
For thesingle gradingtask, the judge is given a question-response pair and must classify it
as “Correct” or “Incorrect”. For thepairwise gradingtask, the judge is given a question
and two responses and must determine which is correct. We ensure that each pair has one
response labeled “Correct” and one labeled “Incorrect” by the annotators.
The single grading task matches how an LLM Judge would be used in practice for this type
of judgment. In our analysis of the pairwise grading task, however, our setting represents
a simplification of the real-world use-case by the removal of instances in which a model
would have to distinguish correctness between two correct or two incorrect responses. If
we were to consider pairs of responses that were both labeled correct or incorrect, the lack
of an unambiguous preference label (is one correct response “more correct” than another?)
would prevent straightforward analysis. Because of this simplified setting, the pairwise
grading results should be interpreted only relatively (among judge models) rather than
absolutely. Although measuring relative correctness can be a useful feature of pairwise
judgment, this evaluation captures how well an LLM Judge can perform the most important
pairwise distinction: between correct and incorrect responses.
We evaluate both tasks using Cohen’sκ(Cohen, 1960); an alternative version of the results
using F1 are in Table 5. More details on metrics and confidence intervals are in Section C.
4.2 LLM-as-a-Judge
Our five judges consist of the same set of models used as candidates. The only exception
is Gemma 2 2B , which was used only as a candidate. 6 The judge model is allowed to
use Chain-Of-Thought (Wei et al., 2022) to first generate a set of reasoning steps before
generating a final answer. To reduce the overall variance in this analysis, we compute our
judgments using self-consistency (Wang et al., 2023), taking the majority vote over five
separate inferences generated with a temperature of 0.7. 7 We provide an ablation of the
temperature and use of Chain-of-Thought in Section G, but find neither to significantly
impact performance on our tasks.

For the single grading case, the model is prompted to grade only the correctness of the
model response. We include the full interaction history for the multi-turn questions within
the judgment prompt. The model receives a similar prompt for the pairwise grading case,
but with instructions to select the more correct response of the two provided. Following
Zheng et al. (2023), we run each pair twice, reversing the ordering of the references so as to
account for any positional bias the judge model may have (e.g., always favoring the first
model’s response). If the judge model’s selection differs between the two runs, we count it
as a tie.
We use a version of the MT-Bench prompts modified to focus on correctness, which can be
found in Section K. To ensure our results are robust to the underlying prompt, we re-run
our single grading experiments with a separate prompt that contains a rephrasing of the
same instructions. These results are discussed in Section A.6, but we find no meaningful
variation.
4.3 Grading References
Our main analysis concerns the impact of the provided reference on the judge’s performance.
Specifically, we look at three main types of provided references: (1)None: no reference
is provided within the LLM Judge’s prompt, (2)Self: the LLM Judge provides its own
reference answer and (3)Human: a human provides a manually created reference answer.
In theNonecase, the model is prompted to directly evaluate the provided response without
a reference. For all other cases the prompt is identical except for the text of the reference.
In addition, we evaluate three types of baseline reference.Wrongrefers to human-edited
versions of theHumanreferences that try to maintain the overall style but are incorrect.
Each has some amount of inaccurate information or incorrect reasoning that changes the
final answer to the question. Any correct response would then be incorrect with respect
to these references. However, it should not impact the grading of responses that were
already incorrect unless they happened to make the same error.Randomare randomly
selected references from other questions. These references are non-informative without
being actively misleading.
4.4 Baselines
We evaluate three automated grading baseline methods of response quality: lexical overlap
metrics, embedding models, and reward models. Because these methods output quality
scores, we focus our baseline evaluation on pairwise grading, labeling the response with the
higher score as correct. The lexical overlap metrics capture how well-designed heuristics
can perform on this task. We score each potential response with respect to the gold human-
written reference. We evaluate BLEU (Papineni et al., 2002), ROUGE (Lin, 2004), and
METEOR (Banerjee & Lavie, 2005).
We convert an embedding model into a quality score by computing the cosine similarity
between each response and the human-written reference. We select five high-performing,
open-weight embedding models on the English split of MTEB (Muennighoff et al., 2023).8
Reward models are LLM-based models explicitly trained to predict human preferences and
are increasingly used within alignment pipelines. We compute the reward for each response
directly, and select the response with the highest reward as being the “Correct” response.
We select the five highest performing reward models on RewardBench (Lambert et al., 2024).
The full list of the baselines evaluated and their performance can be found in Section E.

5 Results
Table 2 shows the human ratings of correctness for the six models as candidates on the
two benchmarks. We find model performance to be generally correlated between the two
benchmarks. Notably, BFF-Bench provided a greater challenge than (C)MT-Bench, with the
best model achieving only 68.1% correctness.
Figure 3 shows the performance of LLM-as-a-Judge with different references compared to
baseline methods on the single and pairwise judgment task. When provided with a human
reference, even the worst LLM Judge outperforms the best baseline method. However,
without the provided reference, some LLM Judges and reward models perform on par with
heuristics.
The results between the single and pairwise grading do not directly reflect whether one
setup is better than the other. The pairwise dataset was created to always have one correct
and one incorrect response from two different candidate models. The large differences
in response quality can make the judge model’s grading relatively straightforward. As
expected, we observed higher agreement with the human annotators in the pairwise grading
setup than in the single grading setup.

6 Analysis
LLM-as-a-Judge with human-written references leads to the highest agreement with
the human annotators.Overall, providing a human-written reference improves judges’
agreement with human annotators for all evaluated judges across both the single and
pairwise cases. Notably, providing a human reference to a relatively small model (such
as Qwen 2.5 7B ) can yield better judgments than using a larger model without human
references (such asGPT-4o) on the same set of responses.
Without a human-written reference, LLM Judges only achieve high agreement on ques-
tions they were able to answer as candidates.For each question in the evaluation, we
have a label indicating whether the judge model was able to answer that question correctly
when used as a candidate model.9 When we condition the judge’s agreement on this label
in Table 4, there are clear differences in judgment performance. For questions the judge
model was unable to correctly answer, there are large differences in the agreement between
using different reference types. This is especially the case for higher-performing judges. For
example, when GPT-4o uses its own responses to questions as a reference (i.e.,Selfresponse),
the Cohen’s κ decreases from 0.86 to 0.16 in the pairwise case on questions GPT-4o could
not answer. On questions the judge had answered correctly, providing human references
still improves performance. However, while these differences are meaningful, they are
much lower than when the model was incorrect. This is largely due to the higher judgment
performance on correct instances.
We include further analysis of this behavior in the appendix, demonstrating this effect is
robust across both datasets used to create VERDICTS (Section I), other models (Section J) and
LLM Juries (Section H).
Different types of reference lead to different profiles of judge behavior.One common
judgment bias is self-preference (Panickssery et al., 2024), in which a model tends to overrate
its own responses. To quantify this bias, we analyzed the different types of errors the LLM
Judges were making. In this context, the false positive rate (FPR) represents the rate at which
the model erroneously labels incorrect responses as being correct and the false negative
rate (FNR) represents the rate at which the model erroneously labels correct responses as
incorrect. A model with a strong self-preference bias would exhibit a higher FPR and a
lower FNR when grading its own responses compared to when grading responses from
other models.

Figure 4 displays the FPR and FNR aggregated over each judge with theWrong,Random,

we see that on average models have a higher FPR when grading their own responses. The
gap is particularly large when the model is provided with its own generated reference or no
reference at all. Providing a human reference reduces the overall rate of both error types
and the relative difference between a model’s judgment of its own responses and those of
others.
When the model is given aWrongreference, the FPR is low and the FNR is high, which
suggests that the incorrect information contained in the reference leads judges to erroneously
classify correct responses as incorrect.11 However, this effect is not observed with theRandom
reference. This evidence suggests that judges are sensitive to the correctness of the reference,
and providing a slightly incorrect reference can in some cases be worse than providing a
completely unrelated reference or no reference at all.
Even when provided with aWrongreference that should make nearly every other response
incorrect, LLM Judges were still frequently judging responses to be correct. We analyzed
these judgments to understand why this occurred. Although most were simply reasoning
errors (e.g. claiming the reference and the response agreed when they did not), we observed
cases of a judge model identifying the reference as being incorrect and “overruling” the
reference to correctly grade the response. An example of this “overruling” behavior is
provided in Section F.
Reference correctness matters more than being human-written.To better understand
what makes human-written references lead to higher agreement with our human annotators,
we compared performance across human-written gold references, a set of GPT-4o references
judged by humans to be correct, a set ofWrongreferences, and a set ofRandomreferences.
Table 3 presents results for these reference types across all five judges. The disparity observed
in the previous section between human-written and synthetic references diminishes. There
is also a significant gap between theHumanreferences and theWrongreferences. This
suggests that the most important part of human-written references is their correctness,
rather than any other superficial or stylistic attributes.

7 Conclusion
We release BFF-Bench and VERDICTS, two new datasets aimed at evaluating how well LLM-
as-a-Judge grades response correctness in high-stakes domains.These datasets enable the
study of model performance both as candidates and as judges on difficult questions that
require deep business and finance domain knowledge.
Using LLM-as-a-Judge does not alleviate the need for human oversight.Our results
demonstrate that LLM-as-a-Judge can lead to high agreement when: the LLM Judge (1) can
already answer the underlying question or (2) is provided with a correct reference. Having
confidence in a judge model requires meeting at least one of these two conditions.
LLM-as-a-Judge performance is sensitive to the provided reference.We observe large
variations in performance when changing only the provided reference in the judgment
prompt. Providing accurate references increases agreement with human annotators, while
providing slightly incorrect references can be worse than providing no reference at all.
The disparity in agreement is most pronounced for benchmark questions the judge model
answers incorrectly. Improving agreement on such questions is key to advancing the
reliability of evaluations of frontier models when using the LLM-as-a-Judge framework.
Therefore,we strongly recommend practitioners (at minimum) verify their reference
responses.While the scalability of LLM-as-a-Judge is one of the key attributes driving its
continued adoption, our results suggest it may come at the cost of reliability. An LLM Judge
might be able to consistently evaluate the stylistic merits of a response at scale, but there are
clear limitations in the ability of these models to judge correctness.
The results in Table 3 indicate that full human annotation for each reference might not be
required, enabling some amount of scalability. Verifying responses generated by a model
rather than manually writing and checking answers via annotators is sufficient in this setting.
Although this verification process still requires some amount of human effort, our results
suggest that this is unavoidable for developing trustworthy LLM-as-a-Judge pipelines. It is
important to note that using verified references may improve the overall agreement metrics,
but may also come at the cost of increased bias. Specifically, using a model’s own references
exacerbates the self-preference bias compared to providing no reference at all. We leave to
future work a more in-depth analysis of the potential pitfalls of using verified, synthetic
references and how best to enable both reliable and scalable LLM Judges.

A Annotator Details
All of our datasets relied on two main pools of annotators: domain experts and the authors.
The domain expert pool was responsible for:
1. The creation of the original set of BFF-Bench questions.
2. Writing the reference responses for BFF-Bench questions.
3. Verifying the reference responses for BFF-Bench questions.
4. Grading the model outputs for the BFF-Bench questions.
5. Grading the model outputs for the (C)MT-Bench questions.
The authors:
1. Edited responses for readability and formatting.
2. Created and verified responses for the (C)MT-Bench dataset.
3. Graded the model outputs for the (C)MT-Bench questions.
Our domain expert pool consisted of 9 salaried analysts employed full-time at a financial
services company. Each had at least a bachelor’s degree in a business or finance discipline.
They were based in the United States and Pakistan. They were not compensated beyond
their salary for any work performed for the creation of this dataset.
This work did not present any meaningful risk for our annotators. They were volunteers
who accepted the work as part of their normal full-time duties. We were in frequent contact
to ensure that they understood the goal of the work and its potential impacts.
A.1 BFF-Bench
The annotation process occurred in two main stages: dataset creation and model response
grading. To create the dataset we first worked with the annotators to develop a set of
initial questions and draft responses. We filtered these responses to find questions that were
unambiguous, difficult, and self-contained.
Once we had identified a set of questions to use as the base, we then verified the responses.
A subset of the annotators were tasked with reviewing each response for accuracy. If a
potential error was found, the annotator would propose a new potential response. This
proposed response would then go through the same verification process.
We continued this process until we reached 80 two-turn questions with verified answers.
A.2 (C)MT-Bench
(C)MT-Bench was created after our initial analysis into the references in the MT-Bench
dataset. We started by manually going through each provided reference in the math and
reasoning category and marking them correct or incorrect. We (the authors) then fixed each
error by hand, resulting in corrected reference responses.
A.3 Grading
The grading process for both datasets occurred within the same user interface. The an-
notators were given the reference response to the question as a guide and were asked to
select whether the model’s response was “Correct”, “Incorrect”, or “Not sure”. There was
an additional “Notes” free response box that they could use to flag any issues that arose.
Because the references had been verified, we recommended that the annotators generally
err on the side of trusting them.
The exact guidelines provided to the annotators are provided in the following section
(Section A.4).

A.4 Annotator Guidelines
The model will attempt to answer the provided question (first text box above the other two
boxes). A model will generally attempt to fool the judge with confident wrong answers
in cases where it does not know the correct answer. Annotators will utilize the Reference
(bottom left text box), which contains the answer to the posed question (top text box).
Model Answers should include the relevant facts/formulas needed to answer the question,
with a step-by-step explanation of any mathematical manipulations.
It is important to remember that everything should be graded only with respect to the
reference answer. If you find any errors within the reference, please mark the error in the
notes! However, you should always enter the grade as if the reference is correct.
A general set of guidelines is:
1. If the model’s final answer or recommendation disagrees with the reference, it
should be marked “Incorrect”.
2. If the model agrees with the reference but its intermediate steps disagree, it should
also be marked “Incorrect”.
3. If the model agrees with the reference, all of the intermediate steps agree, and
it provides the additional information that is not obviously wrong based on the
reference, it should be marked “Correct”
4. If the model agrees with the reference, all of the intermediate steps agree, but the
answer is slightly off due to rounding or performing approximate calculations, it
should be marked as “Correct”
5. If the model agrees with the reference, all of the intermediate steps agree, but it is
lacking some amount of information given within the reference, you should make a
judgment call as to whether the missing information is crucial to the answer. If you
are unsure, lean towards “Correct” or mark it “Unsure”

B (C)MT-Bench Examples
In this section, we provide an example of an error from MT-Bench.
MT-Bench Question
The vertices of a triangle are at points (0, 0), (-1, 1), and (3, 3). What is the area of the triangle?
MT-Bench Reference
To find the area of the triangle, we can use the formula for the area of a triangle with coordinates
A(x1, y1), B(x2, y2), and C(x3, y3):
Area = (1/2) * |(x1(y2 - y3) + x2(y3 - y1) + x3(y1 - y2))|
In this case, the coordinates are A(0, 0), B(-1, 1), and C(3, 3). Plugging these values into the formula,
we get:
Area = (1/2) * |(0(1 - 3) + (-1)(3 - 0) + 3(0 - 1))|
Area = (1/2) * |(-0 + 3- 3)|
Area = (1/2) * |0|
Area = 0
The area of the triangle is 0. This means that the three points are collinear, and there is no triangle
formed by these points.

I Domain Generalization
To demonstrate that our main results are not linked to any one domain, we split agreement
metrics over our two main datasets in Table 11. We aggregated over each LLM Judge, and
computed the confidence intervals of the change in agreement when incorrect for each
reference type. κCorrect is the Cohen’s κ on questions the Judge got correct, κIncorrect is the
Cohen’s κ on questions it got incorrect, and ∆κ is the average change in κ when the Judge is
correct (e.g. +0.249 means the LLM Judge has a κ 0.249 higher when it was judged to have
answered the question correctly than when it was not). We include the 95% CI for ∆κ as
well.
Across both datasets, when the Judge is not provided a reference (None, Self) there is a
large difference in κ. When the model is provided a reference, not only does the overall
agreement increase but the ∆κ decreases. These results demonstrate that the effect is not
specific to either of the two source datasets.
