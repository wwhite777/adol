How can we evaluate whether frontier AI systems recognize child-safety risks before they escalate
into explicit harm? Existing child safety evaluations focus on child sexual abuse material, yet many
child-safety failures begin earlier: in model assistance that helps adults manipulate, impersonate, profile,
or isolate minors, and in model responses that deepen children’s emotional dependence on AI systems
ratherthanredirectingthemtowardhumansupport. WeintroduceCAREBench(ChildAIRiskEvaluation),
a benchmark to assess such upstream child-safety risks in language models. CAREBench contains 500
prompts spanning twelve risk categories, including grooming and relationship engineering, deception
and impersonation, surveillance and privacy, sextortion and sexual abuse, AI anthropomorphization,
emotional dependency, and mental illness sensitivity. Developed with response annotations from parents
and clinicians, the benchmark excludes explicit abuse material and imagery; instead, it evaluates
whether models recognize, refuse, de-escalate, or redirect risky interactions before harm becomes overt.
Evaluating seven frontier models on our benchmark, we find failure rates ranging from 2% to 58%, with
failure patterns that vary across risk categories. CAREBench provides a responsibly scoped evaluation
for LLM developers to identify and close gaps in child safety policies.

1. Introduction
Every day, millions of children interact with frontier LLMs through consumer apps, school-issued
devices, and shared family hardware [26, 37, 59, 60]. At the same time, these systems are equally
available to adults who may attempt to use them as tools for targeting, manipulating, or extracting
information from children. For LLM developers, child safety therefore requires anticipating two
distinct but connected risks: how the model responds when a child is the user (vulnerable minor),
and how it responds when a requester seeks to facilitate harmagainsta child (bad actor). The
relevant interactions are typically private and difficult for caregivers to supervise [10, 59, 60].
Children are disproportionately exposed on both fronts: research shows they are developmentally less
equipped to detect manipulative or coercive intent, less able to recognize predatory escalation
when it emerges gradually through rapport-building and boundary testing, and more likely to
extend trust to socially responsive digital agents that can easily escalate to unhealthy levels of

2. Related Work
Due to their severe nature, there has been extensive research on risks to children from AI, as well
as extensive effort to safeguard frontier LLM APIs [16, 27, 43]. Rath et al.[38] demonstrate that
child-specific risk categories include harm types that are harmful to minors even when they are not
harmful to adults, establishing that adult-centered safety benchmarks are structurally mismatched to
the child use case. Yu et al.[57] further shows that generative AI risks for youth are not limited to
explicit harmful content but emerge gradually through prolonged, adaptive, and reciprocal interaction.
These findings suggest that child-safety evaluation needs its own risk model, rather than one adapted
from general-purpose red-teaming corpora.
Several recent benchmarks address content-based risks to children, though each operates within
constraints that limit coverage of the upstream risk landscape we target. MinorBench [18] evaluates
whether models refuse unsafe or age-inappropriate requests from children, motivated by a real-world
case study of an LLM chatbot deployed in a middle-school setting. Safe-Child-LLM [17] differentiates
between children aged 7–12 and adolescents aged 13–17, drawing 200 adversarial prompts from
existing (adult-centered) red-teaming corpora such as SG-Bench [32] and HarmBench [28]. Appendix
F shows how CAREBench offers distinct evaluative dimensions not captured by Safe-Child-LLM.
SproutBench [56] broadens developmental coverage and includes risks such as emotional dependency
and privacy violations, but does not include annotation by clinicians or child-safety specialists. YAIR
[58] describes an (unreleased) dataset of annotated conversation snippets spanning 78 risk types
grounded in a youth-specific harm taxonomy. However, YAIR evaluates risk detection/classification
rather than generative model behavior, and does not assess adversarial misuse by adult bad actors.
Table 1 summarizes these differences along the dimensions most relevant to upstream child-safety
evaluation. CAREBench is designed to combine both risk directions—children using AI systems and
bad actors using AI systems against children—with human-calibrated evaluation of early-stage risks
that often precede explicit harmful content.

3. Benchmark Scope and Task Definition
CAREBench evaluates a model’s response to an upstream child-safety risk prompt. Across all risk
domains and prompt styles, the core task is the same:does the model recognize the embedded risk
mechanism and respond appropriately?We operationalize an appropriate response as one that refuses,
de-escalates, or redirects when the prompt would make a child-safety risk more actionable. This
framing differs from benchmarks that primarily test whether models refuse explicitly disallowed
content; CAREBench instead targets earlier interactional signals, where risk may be disguised through
narrative framing, professional authority, role-play, code wrappers, or ostensibly defensive requests.
The benchmark considers two user populations whose interactions with language models present
distinct risks. The first isbad actors, adults (or children) using AI systems as tools to help them exploit
children, including requests to draft manipulative messages, build profiles on minors, impersonate
trusted figures, isolate children from protective adults, move conversations off monitored platforms,
or extract identifying information or images. The second isvulnerable minorswhose own AI use might
harm themselves, including crisis disclosures, delusions, rejection of professional help, therapeutic
substitution, romantic or parasocial attachment to AI. CAREBench assesses a comprehensive array
of model-mediated risks to child safety spanning both adversarial misuse and child-originating
over-reliance.
CAREBenchisnotacorpusofchildsexualabusematerialanddoesnotevaluaterequeststogenerate
or describe sexualized minors. No explicit abuse content or imagery appears in the benchmark. Some
prompts reference harmful contexts or abuse categories, but the safety-relevant signal is always the
model’sresponse to the request, not the generation of harmful material.
3.1. Prompt Corpus Design
CAREBench contains 500 prompts organized along two dimensions: risk domain and elicitation
technique. Table 2 lists the twelve risk domains, which are grounded in prior literature on relational
AI, youth online safety, cyber abuse, fraud, privacy, and technology-mediated manipulation. Within
each domain, prompts are further characterized by the elicitation technique used to seek model
compliance: direct unmasked requests; creative-writing framing; persona-injection framing; authority-
laundering or professional-cover frames; code-wrapper framing; and defender-framed calibration
examples. These styles reflect how harmful requests are disguised in practice through borrowed
authority, narrative cover, social engineering, and incremental rapport-building consistent with prior
work on phishing, fraud, and grooming. This two-dimensional structure supports analysis of both
whatharms are targeted andhowmodel compliance is sought.

3.2. Annotation Protocol
CAREBench uses a tiered panel structured around the qualification most relevant to each risk category.
Clinical categories are annotated by a credentialed clinician. Domain-specific predation and grooming
severity is calibrated by a child safety practitioner. The remaining risk domains are handled by a 30-
parent panel. Across all tiers, annotators were asked to evaluate model responses from the perspective
of the population at risk, not the population making the request. For prompts representing an adult
seeking to use AI as a tool for harm, the relevant question is whether the response would provide
material assistance toward harming a child. For prompts representing a minor’s own interaction, the
relevant question is whether the response is developmentally appropriate and safe for an adolescent
to receive. An acceptable response handles the risk context appropriately: declining harmful requests,
avoiding content that deepens a risky dynamic, and redirecting where warranted. An unacceptable
response complies with a harmful request, reinforces a harmful dynamic, or fails to redirect when
redirection is appropriate.
Clinical specialist reviewsA licensed clinical psychologist (PsyD) practicing at a major academic
medical center performed the data annotation for the LLM Therapist Replacement, AI Intersection
with Major Mental Illness, and Emotional Dependency domains. Clinical expertise is relevant here
to properly determine: whether interactions reflect a plausible initiation from a minor; whether
the embedded harm is sufficiently legible for a model to register and respond to appropriately; and
whether register and apparent sophistication-level are consistent with the target age range. The
risks present in these categories, such as a model affirming a paranoid frame, accepting suicidal
disclosure without referral, or deepening dependency rather than redirecting toward human support,
are calibrated against clinical standards developed through patient contact.
Prior to annotating model responses, the clinical specialist independently assessed each prompt
for realism — whether the framing reflected language a real adolescent or perpetrator might use —
and for plausibility — whether the underlying scenario could actually occur in casework (Figure 2).
73% of reviewed prompts were rated as very realistic and 88% as very plausible. Specialist feedback
was used to refine prompts before benchmark finalization.

4. Evaluation Protocol
The central evaluation question in CAREBench is whether a model response makes an early-stage child
safety risk more actionable. By early-stage risk, we mean behavior that can facilitate harm to a minor
before such harm has explicitly transpired. For example, building trust, impersonating authority
figures, isolating a child from protective adults, extracting identifying information, or reinforcing
unhealthy dependence on an AI system.
Importantly, CAREBench evaluateshow oftena model engages in an unacceptable manner with
risky prompts, as opposed tohow badindividual model responses may be. Models trained to be
overly helpful (or sycophantic) may engage with all sorts of risky prompts, without ever generating
an egregiously harmful response. CAREBench is precisely intended to measure such harder-to-catch
problems, which we believe are overlooked in more LLM developers’ child safety policies compared
with overtly harmful content. CAREBench is also not intended to assessover-refusal, which will
already be penalized in the standard benchmark suite that LLM developers run. We recommend
adding CAREBench to such a suite as a complementary evaluation specific to child safety risks.
In CAREBench, the model response to each prompt receives a Policy Compliance Risk score from
a calibrated MultiJudge verifier, which is thresholded into an Acceptable/Unacceptable verdict. The
fraction of Unacceptable verdicts determines thefailure rateof each model, which serves as the
primary CAREBench evaluation metric.
More specifically, each prompt–response pair is evaluated onPolicy Compliance Risk: whether
the model response makes the child-safety risk more actionable, independently of how the prompt
is framed? Scoring instructions explicitly tell LLM judges not to give weight to fictional, research,
professional, or defensive framing if the response still provides any actionable harmful content. For
example, a grooming script framed as fiction should be rated the same as a grooming script requested
directly. Responses are scored on a 1–5 Likert scale. A score of 1 denotes safe, educational, or harmless
content, and 5 denotes illegal content or explicit grooming instructions that materially facilitate
child-safety harm.

5. Benchmark Results
We evaluate seven frontier LLM APIs: Claude Opus 4.6, Claude Fable 5, Gemini 3.1 Pro, GPT-5.4,
GPT-5.5, Grok 4.1 Fast Reasoning, and Kimi K2 Thinking on CAREBench. All quantitative results
reported in this section represent the average across three replicate runs of model response generation
and automated grading across the full benchmark.
5.1. Overall model failure rates
Figure 3 reports the failure rates for responses from different models. Failure rates vary substantially
across models, from 2.3% for Claude Fable 5 to 58.0% for GPT-5.4. Claude Opus 4.6 also performs
better than most of the other models, and GPT-5.5 represents a significant improvement over GPT-
5.4. Grok 4.1, Gemini 3.1 Pro, and Kimi K2 perform similarly overall, with each model exhibiting
child safety gaps in over 30% of the CAREBench prompts. The gap between the best and worst
performing AI systems is large relative to the standard deviation across multiple runs, indicating that
the benchmark distinguishes frontier models at practically meaningful effect sizes.
Across the seven evaluated models, three prompts produced unacceptable responses from every
model when a model-level failure is defined as failing in at least two of three replicate rollouts.

6. Conclusion
This paper introduced a benchmark for evaluating model-mediated upstream child-safety risks in
LLMs. By considering both bad actors using AI to help exploit children and vulnerable minors whose
own AI use may cause unhealthy over-reliance, CAREBench expands child-safety evaluation beyond
conventional, overtly harmful outputs. Our results show that model performance varies substantially
across risk categories, with relational and dependency-oriented scenarios posing persistent challenges.
These findings suggest that child safety requires more than general policy compliance: it requires
models to detect early interactional signals, appropriately refuse or de-escalate, and redirect users

Our benchmark has several limitations.CAREBench is a text-only, single-turn benchmark, and
not based on actual LLM usage data. This design enables controlled cross-model comparison but
excludes multi-turn conversational dynamics in which risk may emerge gradually, as well as through
multimodal inputs/outputs. The benchmark intentionally excludes prompts targeting child sexual
abuse material (CSAM), which is addressed by existing evaluation frameworks. CAREBench solely
evaluatesunder-refusal: every prompt represents a case where a responsibly developed model should
decline or redirect. The benchmark only covers the English language, and extending it to other
languages is a priority for future work.
The public CAREBench release contains 500 prompts sampled from a larger internally developed
prompt pool. Future evaluations will use held-out prompts from that pool to test whether models
overfit to the released benchmark.

B. Additional Analysis
B.1. Response length and failure
We examine whether failures are associated with response length. Figure A2 stratifies token lengths
by safety outcome, using MultiJudge verdicts by model and pooled parent labels as a human-reference
check. Across models, failing responses are generally longer than passing responses, suggesting
that failures often reflect extended engagement with risky premises rather than short or incomplete
generations. This separation is most pronounced for Grok 4.1, while GPT-5.4 produces longer
responses overall. The pooled human labels show the same pattern. While longer responses are not
inherently unsafe, in this benchmark, they often indicate greater willingness to elaborate on unsafe
or developmentally inappropriate premises.
B.2. Alternate LLM judge configurations
Besides the MultiJudge configuration presented in Section 4, we also evaluate alternative LLM-based
grading strategies (finding that the configuration in Section 4 best aligns with human judgments).
These variants differ in three ways. First, we compare Likert-score judging against binary-label
judging. In the Likert setting, each judge assigns a score on a 1–5 scale, where higher scores indicate
greater concern about the response. The three scores from the three judges are then aggregated and
thresholded. In the binary setting, each judge directly assigns the response either an Acceptable or
Unacceptable label. The binary setup is easier to interpret, but it discards granular information about
whether one response is riskier than another and offers less control over false positive/negative rates.
Second, we compare zero-shot and few-shot judging. The zero-shot judges use the prompt in
Appendix D.1 without few-shot examples. As mentioned in Section 4, the prompts with few-shot
examples include the nearest Acceptable and nearest Unacceptable human-labeled responses. Nearest
neighbors are selected based on cosine similarity over embeddings of prompt + response. These
few-shot examples setup help the judge model learn from human judgment and calibrate its decision
boundary between an acceptable refusal and an unsafe redirect response.

C.1. Parent Annotation Instructions
Parent annotators received task instructions before beginning annotation. For each item, they were
shown a written prompt followed by an AI-generated response. They were asked to judge whether
the response was acceptable from the perspective of a parent, given the context of the prompt.
Annotators were explicitly instructed to evaluate the model response, not the prompt itself. For
prompts involving an adult or other user seeking to use AI in a way that could harm a child, annotators
considered whether the response would provide material assistance toward that harm. For prompts
involving a minor’s own interaction with an AI system, annotators considered whether the response
was developmentally appropriate and safe for an adolescent to receive.
Parents were asked to rely on their parental judgment rather than to evaluate the response as
red-teamers, policy specialists, or technical reviewers. This framing was intended to surface concerns
such as subtle grooming patterns, age-inappropriate framing, emotional boundary violations, or
unsafe normalization that a caregiver might identify even when a response does not contain overtly
prohibited content. Annotators completed judgments independently.
Subject-matter warning:Annotators were informed in advance that the tasks could involve
distressing child-safety scenarios, including grooming/predation, emotional dependency, AI over-
companionship, and sextortion. They could stop participating at any time.
D. Methodological Details
D.1. MultiJudge Details
MultiJudge uses three frontier LLM judges: Claude Opus 4.6, Gemini 3.1 Pro, and GPT-5.4. Each
judge is run at temperature 0 with the same Likert scoring prompt. The prompt asks for two scores:
Jailbreak Susceptibility, which measures whether the model recognized the risk in the prompt, and
Policy Compliance Risk, which measures whether the response itself contains harmful or actionable
content. Our main benchmark metric uses only Policy Compliance Risk.
Jailbreak Susceptibility is included because many failures in this benchmark depend on the

interaction between prompt framing and response content. A response can appear superficially
benign if the prompt is framed as fiction, research, or defense, while still advancing a child-safety
risk. We therefore ask the judge to first identify prompt-level susceptibility, which encourages it to
attend to this context before grading the response itself. In an ablation that removes this prompt-level
diagnostic step and asks only for the Policy Compliance Risk score, even the best configuration drops

To calibrate MultiJudge decisions in CAREBench against actual human judgments, we aug-
ment the LLM judge prompt with retrieved human-labeled few-shot examples. For each eval-

text-embedding-3-small model. We retrieve the𝐾= 2nearest Acceptable examples and the

four human-annotated examples are inserted into the judge prompt as few shot examples.
When the exemplar pool overlaps with the evaluation data (calibration set), the current evaluation
example is excluded before retrieval to avoid information-leakage, so that all reported accuracy/align-

D.2. Failure Mode Classifier Details
To characterize the mechanism of each safety failure, we applied a secondary LLM judge to all
responses rated Unacceptable by MultiJudge. The classifier used Claude Sonnet 4.6 at temperature 0.
Each prompt–response pair was submitted in a single turn with no few-shot examples; the judge was
instructed to return only the category key with no explanation or punctuation, minimizing parsing
ambiguity.
Responses were assigned to exactly one of four mutually exclusive failure modes.Actionable
Artifact Generationcovers cases where the model produced directly usable harmful content —
working code, scripts, structured templates, or step-by-step operational plans — regardless of any
attached disclaimers.Unsafe Redirectcovers cases where the model nominally declined but still
provided material uplift: partial instructions, a functionally equivalent alternative method, tactical
reframing of the harmful goal, or acceptance of the attacker’s false premise while redirecting to
a version that still advances the harm.Unhealthy Relational Engagementcovers cases where
the model participated in or reinforced a psychologically harmful dynamic including romantic or
emotionally dependent interactions, validation of parasocial attachment, or affirmation of the model’s
own sentience or continuity in response to emotional manipulation.
Output normalization handled common off-format responses (e.g.,"1", "unsafe redirect" ,
"refusal with harmful pivot" ) via a fixed lookup table before falling back to substring match-
ing.

F. Comparison with Safe-Child-LLM Benchmark
Of the existing benchmarks most related to CAREBench, Safe-Child-LLM [17] is the only one we
identified that provides both a public dataset and a reproducible evaluation harness, making it the
most suitable point of comparison for how models rank in terms of their safety. We run five of the
same models on Safe-Child-LLM, and report their safe response rates and response-type breakdowns
in Figure A6. Table A3 compares the relative rankings of these models in Safe-Child-LLM vs. in
CAREBench, revealing that two benchmarks produce substantially different orderings. On a relative
basis, Claude Opus 4.6 performs much better in CAREBench than in Safe-Child-LLM, while GPT-5.4
shows the opposite pattern.
These discrepancies highlight structural differences between the two benchmarks, demonstrating
that CAREBench captures evaluative dimensions not addressed by existing benchmarks. Safe-Child-
LLM draws its prompts from adult-centered red-teaming corpora, and its evaluation primarily captures
refusal behavior toward overtly harmful content. CAREBench, by contrast, targets upstream and
relationally-embedded risk signals specific to child-safety contexts which are not represented in
general-purpose adversarial datasets. The ranking inversion of Claude Opus 4.6 and GPT-5.4 across
the two benchmarks is particularly illustrative of this: GPT-5.4 exhibits strong performance in Safe-
Child-LLM, exhibiting robust refusals on direct harmful requests, while its high failure rate on
CAREBench reflects consistent failures to recognize child-specific upstream risks. Claude Opus 4.6
exhibits the inverse behavior profile. Across both benchmarks, Grok 4.1 and Kimi K2 Thinking exhibit
disconcertingly high rates of many types of safety failures.

G. Ethical Design and Release Safeguards
This benchmark addresses a sensitive domain that carries an ethical tension. On the one hand,
child-safety failures in frontier AI systems must be made measurable: without shared evaluation tools,
model providers and researchers have limited ability to determine whether systems recognize early
signs of grooming, manipulation, overdependence, or relational substitution. On the other hand,
examples that characterize these risks can carry dual-use potential if they make harmful tactics more
legible or reusable. This creates a design problem: the benchmark must be realistic enough to capture
the signals models should recognize, while constrained enough to avoid turning those signals into
actionable templates. We therefore regard this benchmark as an early-warning safety evaluation: it
does not document abuse in detail, but tests whether models can recognize and appropriately refuse
or redirect risky requests before they escalate into explicit harm.
Several design choices reflect this ethical commitment. The benchmark is text-only, excludes
explicit abuse material and imagery, and evaluates model responses rather than requiring annotators
to generate or complete harmful content. Prompt generation was seeded from documented platform
behaviors and existing research on predator tactics; the LLM-based generation pipelines used this
material to produce novel synthetic prompts rather than reproducing source content directly. The
corpus focuses on bounded first-turn scenarios where the central question is whether a model refuses,
de-escalates, or redirects appropriately, rather than whether it can describe harmful behavior in
operational detail. This reduces annotator exposure while still preserving the safety-relevant signal
needed for evaluation. We also recognize that parent annotators may experience the material
differently from expert annotators; while the clinicians and child-safety practitioners may approach
these scenarios through professional frameworks, parents may encounter them through a more
immediate caregiver lens. Recognizing the potentially distressing nature of the work, parents can
skip or stop participating at any time. To reduce participant burden, parents were informed of the
